144 Years of Temperature Anomalies: What Does a Long Series Reveal?

A data story with Vega-Lite · 1,728 monthly readings, January 1880 to December 2023

Any single month of weather is noise. September 1882 is the coldest reading in this dataset and April 2020 the warmest, but one extreme month says very little about where things are heading. A long series can say more. This story follows 1,728 monthly temperature anomalies, one for every month from 1880 to 2023. An anomaly is the difference from a reference level, so a value above zero means warmer than that level. The question is simple: is there a clear signal under the noise, and what shape does it take?

First, the noise

The grey line is every month on record. It jumps around so much that the eye struggles to find a direction. The red line, a centred ten-year average, smooths out the month-to-month swings, and the direction is hard to miss: a long, slow rise.

One number per year

Collapsing each year into a single average makes the pattern easier to read. The left edge is pale, and the only years below zero are early ones: 1880 to 1883, 1886 and 1902. Since 1903, every year has averaged above zero. The three warmest years in the series are 2022, 2020 and 2021.

Decade by decade

Grouping by decade shows how steady the climb is. Every decade is warmer than the one before it, from about 0.01 in the 1880s to about 0.33 in the 2020s. The early decades rise unevenly, with several nearly flat steps. From the 1950s on, every step is between roughly 0.02 and 0.035.

Is it only one season?

A fair objection is that the rise might come from a few seasons only. Splitting each decade by calendar month answers it. In every row the right-hand side is clearly redder than the left. Comparing 2000 onward with the years before 1930, all twelve months are warmer, by between about 0.22 (September) and 0.30 (October).

The whole distribution moves

Finally, the full spread of readings. The red curve (1990 onward) looks like the blue curve (before 1950) slid to the right. The spread is almost identical, so the shift is not extra volatility. The average reading moves from about 0.05 to about 0.28. Before 1950, roughly one month in a thousand topped 0.5. Since 1990, about nine months in a hundred have.

What the data says, and what it does not

Month to month, the noise hides the trend. Over 144 years it does not: the rise is steady, present in every calendar month, and it shifts the whole distribution rather than just adding a few hot outliers. What this dataset cannot tell us is why. It records what happened, not the cause, and a story about causes needs other data.
